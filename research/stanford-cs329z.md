# CS329Z — Engineering AI Agents (Stanford University, Fall 2026)

- **URL(s):** https://cs329z.stanford.edu/ (home page with the schedule), https://cs329z.stanford.edu/logistics.html (course info, coursework), https://cs329z.stanford.edu/project.html (course project). Course code: https://github.com/cs329z
- **Instructors:** Diyi Yang, Michael Ryan and John Yang. TAs: Owen Queen, Anusheh Chaudry, Shreyas Sharma, Houjun Liu.
- **Level/prereqs:** Graduate-level CS course (CS 3xx number; the site does not state a level). Prereq: any of CS224N, CS224U, CS224V or CS336, or equivalent background in natural language processing.
- **Format:** In person, 3 units, Autumn quarter. Mon/Wed 1:30-2:50 p.m., Skilling Auditorium, Wed Sep 23 to the Demo Day on Wed Dec 9, 2026. Lectures, two guest lectures and a fireside chat; two homeworks, each followed by a short oral quiz on design decisions; a paper video; a team project.
- **Public materials:** Slides (PDF) for lectures 1-13 (through Nov 4). Public YouTube videos for lectures 1 and 2 only; other recordings are on Canvas (Stanford login). Readings for every session. Public GitHub starter code for HW1 and HW2, handouts (PDF) in those repos, and the lecture 5 demo notebook. Paper Video handout (PDF).
- **License:** The three course repos (assignment1-harness, assignment2-evals, lecture5-frameworks) are MIT; the HW READMEs invite reuse "with brief attribution". No license is stated for the slides or the site.

**Overview.** The course teaches how to engineer agentic systems across the full range from simple LLM pipelines to compound AI systems (LLMs, retrievers, tools, optimizers) to autonomous agents. Students learn to choose problems, decompose them, select components, collect and curate data, build evaluations and reason about design tradeoffs. Core components (RAG, tool use, agent loops) are first built from scratch, then shown as abstractions in frameworks such as DSPy and LangGraph. The schedule runs from foundations and LLM internals through RAG, tool use and MCP, frameworks, design patterns, memory, multi-agent systems and optimization, then data for agents, evaluation and LLM-as-judge, safety and guardrails, and coding and proactive agents. Two fully applied homeworks have students build an agent harness with no framework (tools, terminal sandbox, compaction, memory, human approval, guardrails, simulated-user evaluation) and then author and validate a benchmark task in the Harbor framework. A quarter-long team project must define its evaluation early and report results with baselines and error analysis.
